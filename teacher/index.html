My Granddad was a Bugler · Year 3

Teacher guide

Resource home

A flexible 30–45 minute lesson for Year 3 (ages 8–9). The centre is a person and a family memory, not a battle narrative. Choose activities to suit your class.

Six-page story · Student worksheet (no solutions) · Sources and limitations

Before you begin

Read all six story pages. They mention a parent’s death, cold trenches and Harold being wounded. There are no graphic descriptions. The optional historical photograph shows trench work; preview it before sharing. The story is supplied family narrative, not a verified biography.

Know your class: recent bereavement, displacement, war experience or military deployment may make this topic difficult. Offer an equivalent gentle task without asking children to explain why. Do not simulate battles, trenches or being wounded, and do not use surprise bugle calls.

Learning intentions

  • Explain one Remembrance Day custom using a simple historical fact.
  • Sequence three events from the family story.
  • Distinguish a family recollection from what a photograph or record can establish.
  • Choose a respectful response without needing a personal war connection.

Success sounds like: “I can say why 11 November matters.” “I can tell you where my information came from.” “I can name something we still do not know.”

These are teaching intentions, not a claim of formal curriculum alignment. Check your jurisdiction’s curriculum and school guidance.

Preparation

  • Device and display; headphones only if appropriate. Test sound at low volume, or teach entirely without sound.
  • Print one student sheet per learner if needed; paper and pencils are enough.
  • Preview official external links. They can contain material for older readers.
  • Have a quiet alternative ready: draw a safe place and write one action that helps others feel welcome.

Suggested lesson: 40 minutes

1 · Welcome and choice — 4 minutes

Show Home. Say: “We are learning about remembering people with care. You can listen, draw or be quiet. You do not have to tell us a family story.” Ask: “What can help us remember someone?” Take fictional or everyday examples.

2 · Shared reading — 10 minutes

Read Harold’s six pages with pauses. Introduce bugler and pioneer. Ask what Harold did before joining, and what the bugle meant to his grandchildren. Keep the source label visible. Students may skip trench details or move to Remember together.

Use the narrative as a family account. Do not improvise extra combat scenes, motives or conversations.

3 · Historical context and listening — 7 minutes

Read Why we remember. Stress: the Armistice stopped fighting on the Western Front, not every war everywhere. Explain the poppy and the invitation to a minute’s silence. Complete the date question together.

If desired, compare the single and higher synthetic notes. Explain that these are made by the computer, not Harold’s instrument and not official calls. The authentic Last Post listening link opens the AWM page; preview and supervise it. No listening is compulsory.

4 · Evidence detectives — 8 minutes

Use Activities. Compare the beach recollection with the optional pioneers photograph, or use the story alone. Make two columns: “What it tells/shows us” and “What we still wonder”. A museum caption is information about a photo, not a visible detail.

Explain that an official service record could help test dates, but Harold’s record has not been checked here. The photo is not identified as him. Complete the evidence question or ordering task.

5 · Make and reflect — 8 minutes

Choose one worksheet response: draw a remembering object, write two sentences, or plan a kind action. Children can respond to the story rather than their own lives. Optional quiet reflection can be shorter than a minute. It is not a rehearsal requiring a particular emotional response.

6 · Exit thought — 3 minutes

Ask for one fact, its source, and one question. Invite “pass”. Finish with a present-day act of care: listening to someone, welcoming a classmate or resolving a disagreement calmly.

30-minute route: welcome 3; read 8; context 5; one source task 6; response 6; exit 2. Leave sound and photo optional. 45-minute route: add five minutes for paired vocabulary practice or revising a response using the source.

Differentiation and access

Support

  • Read aloud and pre-teach three words with gestures or oral examples.
  • Offer “First… then… later…” cards for sequencing.
  • Accept pointing, drawing, dictation, a home-language response or partner talk.
  • Use “I notice…” and “I wonder…” sentence starters.

Extend without adding distress

  • Ask why a photograph alone cannot tell us everyone’s feelings.
  • Compare a memory with a date that a record could check.
  • Ask what additional evidence would help, rather than asking children to search war material independently.
  • Explain the difference between an armistice and lasting peace.

Wellbeing, inclusion and cultural care

  • Do not assume Australian birth, a military family, a shared religion or a positive family experience of war.
  • Children with refugee, bereavement or military-family experiences should never become class representatives or be invited to disclose them publicly.
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students and families hold diverse histories and views. Do not ask a child to speak for a community. If extending to local histories or cultural material, consult appropriate local guidance and permissions; this resource has not had community cultural review.
  • Remember civilians and family experiences as questions for care, not invented details about Harold’s family. Do not frame war as an adventure, competition or proof of national superiority.
  • Offer quiet participation with eyes open, drawing, no audio, a break or a trusted adult. Respect those who choose not to join a ceremony or religious expression.
  • No student data is collected. Keep worksheet sharing optional; follow school privacy rules if displaying work.

Assessment for learning

Listen for a correct Western Front explanation, source-aware language (“the family story says…”), one evidence limit, and a thoughtful response. Offer a next step rather than a score. A child may show understanding orally or through a labelled drawing.

Model answers and feedback — open when needed

Self-check tasks

  • Date: fighting stopped on the Western Front. “All wars ended” overgeneralises.
  • Word: an armistice is an agreement to stop fighting.
  • Order: butcher’s boy first; battalion bugler second; grandchildren memory third. On the displayed list: 3, 1, 2.
  • Photo: visible trench work is evidence; it does not identify Harold or reveal everyone’s thoughts.

Worksheet possibilities

Word: “A bugler plays a bugle.” “Remembrance means remembering.” Accept accurate child phrasing.

Date: “Fighting stopped on the Western Front at 11 am on 11 November 1918.” Do not accept “every war ended” without revisiting context.

Family source: “The story says Harold called his grandchildren with the bugle.” Limit: it does not independently prove the date or exact words. Ask who recalls it or whether another source could help.

Photo: “I can see boards on the floor and people working.” The collection caption identifies pioneers, about 1916. Limit: the photograph does not identify Harold or show what happened before and after.

Record: “A service record might help check when he joined.” No checked record is supplied; do not mark the document’s enlistment caption as independently confirmed.

Reflection: “I drew a flower to remember people” or “I can help a new student join our game.” There is no required feeling, religious statement or family disclosure.
